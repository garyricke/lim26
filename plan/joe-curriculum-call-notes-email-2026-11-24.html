1. Don’t build the program around Native culture

  • Take the Native framing out of the structure: no “for Alaska Native and American Indian participants” in the title and summary, no culture question built into every lesson, and no rule that a facilitator must be Native.
  • Keep the stories set in Native communities as examples.
  • When we present it, we say it is sensitive to Native participants.
  • Aggression and passivity stay the pivotal part. You noted Alaska Natives tend toward the quiet side and American Indians can run more aggressive. Lesson 6 (“Quiet, stuffed or exploding?”) carries that.

2. New order for every lesson: introduction, teaching, group work

  • Introduction. The facilitator gets up, introduces themself, makes the topic personal with a bit of their own experience, and goes over the subject.
  • Teaching. The idea or skill for the session, with the Bible passages.
  • Group work. It starts with an example story about someone else, and the group takes it apart using what was just taught.

That flips the Healing Groups pattern, where the story comes first and the teaching after.

3. Move from “them” to “you” slowly

  • Early sessions keep the discussion on the person in the story: “Bobby’s boss treated him miserably, and he avoided him.”
  • Once there’s trust, around the second day, questions start turning personal: “Has this ever happened to you?”
  • By the end it’s mostly about the participant. So the personal exercises that now come early (the two baskets, the family tree) move later, or the early ones become about the story character.

One question: in the four-day format (two lessons a day), “the second day” means personal questions start at Lesson 3. In the weekly format, that’s week three. Does that sound right?
