1. Healing-group advertising — the plan

Robert, your ask on the 14th was 15- and 30-second videos saturating Fairbanks and Anchorage, plus online groups aimed at the Alaska villages, New Mexico and Arizona, possibly running every month. Here's how I'd do it, in the order I'd do it.

First, the good news: we finally have the footage. Until last week every ad we could have made would have used stock imagery or old photos. Between Tree of Life and Saturday's Zion concert I now have participant interviews, Roberta on camera, and 360° footage of the concert — on top of the Camp Perkins interviews. It's still on my phone and the 360 camera; I'm pulling it all down this week. That's enough raw material to cut a set of 15- and 30-second spots that are all real people saying true things, which is the only kind of advertising that works for something like a healing group.

Second, before we spend another dollar, three things need fixing. These are mine to do and they're cheap:

  • There's no Facebook pixel on the site. That means when we run ads, Facebook can't see who registered. It can't learn who to show the ad to, we can't show a second ad to someone who visited and didn't sign up, and at the end we can't say which ad produced which registration. We ran the $250 Zion campaign blind for exactly this reason. It's a small job — I'd do it this week.
  • Registrations aren't counted as a conversion in Google Analytics either. Same patch, same afternoon. After that, "did the ads work" stops being a matter of opinion.
  • The Google Ad Grant is still not claimed. This is $10,000 a month of free Google search advertising for nonprofits — not a discount, free. It shows LIM to people who are already typing things like "trauma healing" or "Christian counseling Fairbanks" into Google. It won't replace video, because search only catches people already looking, but it costs nothing but the application and some setup, and it is worth more than any budget we'd realistically approve. It does come with rules — Google switches it off if the ads are neglected — so it needs someone tending it, and I'm happy for that to be me. Jaylene, where did this land?

Third, the shape of the spending. I'd split it two ways rather than run one big campaign:

  • An always-on layer, small and constant — a modest monthly amount in Fairbanks and Anchorage, running whether or not a group is coming up. Nobody signs up for a trauma healing group the first time they hear of it. The job of this layer is that when Roberta announces a date, the name is already familiar.
  • A burst before each group — roughly two weeks out, aimed tightly at the town it's in, pointing at that group's own page and flyer. Anchorage, Busby and the November Fairbanks group would each get one.

Now the numbers, and these are ours, not my guesses. The Zion campaign finished Saturday and it is the first real measurement LIM has. It spent $249.57 and it put the concert in front of 4,821 people in the Fairbanks area, an average of 5.3 times each — 25,696 impressions in total. That works out to about five cents per person reached, or roughly 1,900 people reached for every $100 spent.

That is the answer to "can we saturate Fairbanks." Yes, and cheaply. For $250 we put a message in front of nearly five thousand people five times over. A second $250 would either reach another few thousand or push the frequency higher on the same ones.

One thing to be careful about before we celebrate. That campaign reported 96 responses at $2.60 each, but a "response" is somebody tapping Interested on a Facebook event for a free concert. It is not 96 people in a room, and a healing group is a far bigger ask than a free Saturday concert — it is two days, it is personal, and it asks someone to admit they are carrying something. Cost per actual healing-group registration will be several times that, and I would rather say so now than have it come as a surprise in November. What the Zion number does prove is the cheap part: getting seen. Whether being seen turns into registrations is what the pixel in the previous section lets us finally measure.

What that buys, in round terms:

  • Lean — about $150/month. One burst before each group, one town at a time, nothing in between. Roughly Zion-sized coverage for whichever town has the next group.
  • Recommended — about $350/month. A small always-on layer in both Fairbanks and Anchorage, plus a Zion-sized burst before each group. This is the level where the LIM name is familiar before Roberta announces a date.
  • Saturation — about $700/month. What Robert actually described on the 14th. Note Anchorage is many times the size of Fairbanks, so the same dollars spread much thinner there; either we accept lighter coverage in Anchorage or we target it tightly rather than town-wide.

Two honest cautions. Busby is a different problem from the Alaska towns — it's small enough that Suzette's own network, the church and word of mouth will almost certainly do more than any ad we buy, so I'd put the money into printing and her hands, not into Facebook. And the online groups aimed at the villages, New Mexico and Arizona are the hardest sell of the lot: the audience is scattered, connectivity varies, and an online group asks more of a person than driving to a church. I'd try it once, deliberately, with its own budget and its own measurement, rather than folding it into the main spend and never knowing whether it worked.
