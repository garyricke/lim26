Executive summary

Turning Away From Wrath is a faith-based anger management program from Lutheran Indian Ministries. It brings together three things that are rarely found in one place: the proven skills of secular anger management (triggers, thinking errors, calming tools, assertiveness), what the Bible teaches about anger, and respect for the cultures of Alaska Native and American Indian people.

It is built the way LIM’s Healing Groups are built. People don’t sit through a lecture. Each lesson opens with a story so people can talk about someone else before they talk about themselves. It moves through pair and small-group discussion, a short teaching, Scripture, a hands-on or art exercise and a body-calming practice, and it closes with one thing to remember. Every lesson asks participants what their own culture, family and church taught them about anger, and nobody is told what “Native culture” is.

The journey: Why am I here → Where did I learn it → What sets me off → What am I telling myself → What’s under the anger → Quiet, stuffed or exploding → My toolbox → Making peace and walking on.

Who it is for: adults who want help with their anger, whether they come on their own, at a pastor’s suggestion, or by court order. Everyone is treated the same. Optional Module A serves people who live with someone’s anger.

Delivery: eight weekly evenings, or a four-day intensive like a Healing Group. It needs two trained facilitators (at least one Alaska Native or American Indian) and 8 to 12 participants.

Recommended rollout: pilot it with volunteers first, as Joe suggested, revise it, and then ask local courts and probation what they would need to accept it. It is not a substitute for a state-approved domestic violence intervention program.

Lesson 1

Why am I here?

What has my anger cost me?

By the end: people feel safe enough to come back. They know anger is a normal, God-given feeling and that what we do with it is learned and can change. Each person has named one thing anger has cost them.

Opening circle 15 min

Welcome everyone by name. Say plainly: “Some of you chose to be here. Some of you were sent. Either way, you’re welcome, and you’ll be treated the same. Nobody is going to lecture you. You decide what you take home.” Build the group agreements together instead of reading rules (see facilitator notes).

Section 1 · Story: “Monday Night” 25 min

Monday Night

Dale was forty-one. He had never been to a group like this, and he didn’t plan to talk. A judge had ordered him to come after a shouting match at a gas station turned into a shove. He took the chair closest to the door and folded his arms.

The leader was a woman named Martha. She had grown up in a small village up the river. She didn’t hand out rules. She poured coffee, asked everyone their name and where their family was from, and told a story about her own father’s temper. Then she asked one question: “What has your anger cost you?”

Nobody said anything for a long time. A young man finally said, “My job.” A woman said, “My sister won’t talk to me.” Dale said nothing.

At the break, Dale stood outside in the cold. Martha came out, stood next to him and didn’t say a word. After a while he said, “My daughter won’t let me see my grandson.” Martha nodded. “That’s a big cost,” she said. “I’m glad you came.”

The next Monday, Dale took a chair in the middle of the room.

Discussion (pairs, then the whole group)
  1. Why do you think Dale sat by the door?
  2. What did Martha do that helped? What did she not do?
  3. Why do you think Dale moved his chair the next week?

Section 2 · Teaching: anger is not the enemy 30 min

Anger is a normal feeling. God made us able to feel it. It tells us something seems unfair, unsafe or wrong. Anger can even push us to do good things, like protect a child or stand up against something unjust.

The problem is not the feeling. The problem is what we do with it. Anger is a feeling. Aggression is an action meant to hurt, scare or control someone. You can feel angry without being aggressive.

Here are four things many people believe that are not true:

  • “I was born this way. I can’t change.” Our bodies react to anger on their own, but what we do next is learned. What is learned can be relearned.
  • “Anger always leads to fighting.” There are other ways to handle it, and you’ll learn them here.
  • “You have to be tough to get what you want.” Being firm and respectful works better than being aggressive, and it doesn’t cost you your relationships.
  • “It’s healthy to let it all out.” Studies show that people who yell or hit things to “vent” just get better at being angry.

Anger has costs. Over time it wears on the heart, blood pressure, sleep and stomach. It wears on families too. Children learn how to handle anger by watching us. And being sorry afterward, even truly sorry, doesn’t undo what was said or done.

Video 1 · “This isn’t a lecture” (2 min). A Native facilitator or Elder welcomes the group: why they came to this work, what the group is and isn’t, and that everyone is treated the same.
Culture question (small groups)Growing up, what did you learn about anger: from your family, your community, your church? Was it OK to show it? Who was allowed to be angry, and who wasn’t?

Exercise · Two baskets 25 min

Draw two baskets (or buckets, or boxes). In the first, draw or write what anger has gotten you, because anger often works in the short term: people back off, you feel strong, you get your way. In the second, draw what anger has cost you: people, jobs, trust, peace, health. Look at both. Which one is heavier? Share as much or as little as you like.

Then fill in the Anger Self-Check privately. Nobody else sees it. You’ll fill it in again in Lesson 8.

Practice · Slow breathing 10 min

Sit comfortably. Close your eyes or look at one spot on the floor. Breathe in slowly through your nose while you count to four. Hold for a moment. Breathe out slowly while you count to six. Do this ten times. If it feels uncomfortable at any point, stop. You are in control.

Closing 10 min

One thing to remember: What is one thing you want to take from today?
Between-session challenge: Each day, notice one time you felt even a little angry. Just notice it. Don’t try to fix it yet.

Lesson 2

Where did I learn it?

Who taught me how to be angry?

By the end: people can see that the way they handle anger was learned, often from family, and sometimes passed down through generations of hurt. Learned habits can end with them.

Section 1 · Story: “Three Generations” 25 min

Three Generations

When Walter was seven, he was sent away to a boarding school. If he spoke his own language there, he was punished. He learned to keep quiet and keep his face blank. As a grown man he was gentle most of the time. But when he drank, the anger he had kept in for years came out all at once, and his family learned to stay out of his way.

His son Gerald swore he would never drink, and he never did. But Gerald yelled. He slammed doors when the bills came. His kids learned to go to their rooms and be very quiet until it passed.

Gerald’s son Tyler is twenty-four now. Last month he punched a hole in the wall when his truck wouldn’t start. When he turned around, his three-year-old daughter was hiding behind the couch with her hands over her ears. Tyler stood very still. He had hidden behind that same kind of couch when he was little.

That night he called his grandfather. “Grandpa,” he said, “how do I make it stop with me?”

Discussion
  1. What did Walter learn about anger? Gerald? Tyler?
  2. Each man handled anger differently. What did they have in common?
  3. What do you think Tyler’s grandfather said to him?

Section 2 · Teaching: anger is learned 30 min

Nobody wakes up one day with an anger problem. We learn how to handle anger mostly by watching the people who raised us. When researchers look at children who blow up at school, they usually find someone at home who blows up too.

Sometimes anger works, at least for a while. When a parent yells, the kids obey. When a man slams the table, the argument ends. So the habit sticks, because it gets a need met. The question is whether there is a better way to meet that need.

Some anger is a coping habit, the way we learned to protect ourselves. That is different from a trigger, which is a reaction to something that reminds us of an old hurt (Lesson 3). Habits need new habits to replace them. Triggers need to be understood.

Many Native families also carry historical trauma: hurt from boarding schools, losing land and language, and broken families, passed from one generation to the next. That isn’t an excuse for hurting people. But it helps explain why anger, silence or drinking can run through a family. Understanding where it came from is the first step to letting it stop with you.

Lesson 3

What sets me off?

What are my triggers, and what are my warning signs?

By the end: people can name two or three of their triggers, describe the stages of the anger cycle, and recognize the early warning signs in their own body.

Section 1 · Story: “Nathan’s First Job” 25 min

Nathan’s First Job

Nathan had just finished high school. He was proud to get a job at the building supply store in town, loading lumber and helping customers. He wanted to save money and move out of his parents’ house, because he and his dad didn’t get along.

His supervisor turned out to be a man who drank. Some mornings Nathan could smell it on his breath. The man was moody and quick to criticize, even though Nathan worked hard and got along with everyone. Nathan knew that feeling. His father drank too. He went quiet, tried harder and stayed out of the man’s way. Nothing was ever good enough.

One Monday, after unloading a whole truck of lumber and cement by himself, Nathan asked for his ten-minute break. The supervisor yelled, “What’s the matter with you, kid? Can’t you do anything right?”

Something welled up in Nathan. He shoved the man, who fell to the ground. “Nothing I do is ever good enough for you!” Nathan shouted. “I quit!”

Later, some coworkers who knew what had been going on talked to Nathan and to the manager. The store moved Nathan to another department instead of firing him.

Discussion
  1. What were Nathan’s triggers? Where did they come from?
  2. Did Nathan know he was being triggered by his past?
  3. Looking back, when could Nathan have seen it coming?
  4. What could he have done differently?

Section 2 · Teaching: triggers and the anger cycle 30 min

A trigger is anything that sets off a strong reaction because it reminds us, often without our knowing, of an old hurt. It can be outside us (a loud voice, someone standing too close, being ignored) or inside us (a memory, replaying an old injustice, a feeling of shame).

When we have been hurt badly, especially more than once, the brain’s alarm system gets more sensitive. It goes off at things that only resemble the old danger. Meanwhile, the thinking part of the brain gets pushed aside. That’s why a trigger can feel so fast and so strong.

Anger usually moves through four stages:

  1. Trigger: something happens.
  2. Build-up: the heart races, muscles tighten, and thinking narrows.
  3. Blow-up: yelling, hitting, slamming, or shutting down and walking out.
  4. After: the body stays stirred up for hours, and we’re primed to go off again. Guilt or shame often follows.

The best time to stop the cycle is during the build-up. To do that, you need to know your warning signs. They come in four kinds: body (hot face, tight chest, clenched jaw), actions (pacing, pointing, raising your voice), feelings (hurt, embarrassed, disrespected) and thoughts (“He did that on purpose,” thoughts of getting even).

Lesson 4

What am I telling myself?

Is my story about what happened true?

By the end: people understand that our anger usually comes from how we read an event, not from the event itself. They can name the common thinking errors and use ABCD to check one.

Section 1 · Story: “The Last Parking Spot” 25 min

The Last Parking Spot

It was thirty below, and Carla’s little boy had a fever. The clinic parking lot was full. She saw a car backing out near the door, put on her blinker and waited. Just as the space opened, a pickup came around the other way and pulled right in.

Carla was furious. He saw me. He saw my blinker. He just doesn’t care. She rolled down her window and yelled at the driver, words she would not want her son to repeat.

She parked far away and carried her boy across the icy lot. At the door, she saw the pickup driver helping an old man with an oxygen tank out of the passenger side. The driver looked up at her, confused. He didn’t seem to know why she had yelled.

Carla went inside with her face burning. She thought about it all day. Had he seen her or not? She would never know. But she knew how fast she had decided he was a bad person.

Discussion
  1. What did Carla tell herself about the driver? Was it true?
  2. Why do you think she was so quick to think the worst?
  3. Has something like this ever happened to you?

Section 2 · Teaching: thinking errors 30 min

Every day we make thousands of quick guesses about what is going on. Most of the time we are right. But when we are tired, hurt or triggered, our guesses get bent toward the worst. These bent guesses are called thinking errors. They happen in a split second, usually without our noticing. Here are six that feed anger:

  • Mind reading: “He did that to disrespect me.”
  • Taking it personally: “She’s late because she doesn’t care about me.”
  • Always and never: “People always walk all over me.”
  • Making it huge: “This ruins everything.”
  • Should rules: “He should never talk to me like that.”
  • Replaying it: going over and over it until you’re angrier than when it happened.

Old beliefs shape our guesses too. Someone who grew up where every argument ended in a fight expects a fight. Someone who was told they were worthless hears an insult where none was meant.

ABCD is a way to check your thinking:

  • A · Activating event: what actually happened? (Just the facts a camera would see.)
  • B · Belief: what did I tell myself about it?
  • C · Consequence: how did I feel, and what did I do?
  • D · Dispute: is my belief true? What else could explain it? What’s the worst, best and most likely answer?

Lesson 5

What’s under the anger?

What hurt, fear or shame is hiding underneath?

By the end: people understand anger as often a “cover” feeling over hurt, fear or shame. They can tell righteous anger from sinful anger, and they know where to go for deeper healing.

Section 1 · Story: “Ray Comes Home” 25 min

Ray Comes Home

Ray served two tours in the Army. When he came home, everyone said he was a hero. He found work, fixed up his mom’s house and never complained. He also never talked about what happened over there.

But Ray was angry all the time. When his kids got loud, he yelled at them to shut up. When a tire went flat on the highway, he punched the dashboard so hard he cracked it. His wife, Annie, started walking on eggshells. The kids stayed in their rooms.

One fall day his uncle Joe, who had served in Vietnam, asked Ray to help him at the smokehouse. They worked for hours without talking much. Then his uncle said, “When I came home, I was mad at everything. Took me years to figure out I wasn’t really mad. I was scared, and I was sad about the guys who didn’t come back. Being mad was easier.”

Ray didn’t answer. But he didn’t leave either. They kept working until dark.

Discussion
  1. What do you think is under Ray’s anger?
  2. Why might anger feel “easier” than fear or sadness?
  3. What did his uncle do that might help Ray?

Section 2 · Teaching: the iceberg 30 min

Anger is like the tip of an iceberg. People see the anger. Underneath, where no one sees, are bigger feelings: hurt, fear, shame, grief, loneliness, embarrassment, feeling disrespected or helpless. Anger can feel safer than those feelings. It makes us feel strong when we really feel weak.

The sad part is that when we lead with anger, nobody sees the pain behind it. They only see someone who is scary. We get judged by our anger instead of by what is causing it, and we don’t get the care we need.

Righteous anger and sinful anger. The Bible shows that some anger is right. Jesus was angry when people were being hurt or God was being dishonored. Righteous anger is aimed at what is wrong, not at hurting a person. It comes from love, stays under control, and tries to fix things. Sinful anger is about me: my pride, my getting even. It hurts people, holds grudges and tries to control others. Most of our everyday anger, if we’re honest, is the second kind.

Lesson 6

Quiet, stuffed or exploding?

How can I speak up without blowing up?

By the end: people can name four ways of handling conflict (passive, aggressive, passive-aggressive, assertive), see their own pattern, and practice saying a hard thing calmly and respectfully.

Section 1 · Story: “Lorraine’s Job” 25 min

Lorraine’s Job

Lorraine worked the front desk at the clinic for three years. She was good at it. She knew every family by name and stayed late when it got busy. When a new manager came, he never thanked her. At meetings he gave other people credit for her ideas.

Lorraine didn’t say anything. In her family you didn’t complain, and you didn’t make trouble for people in charge. She just worked harder and went home tired and hurt.

Then the clinic cut her position. Now she was angry, really angry. She avoided the manager at the store. She stopped talking to a coworker she thought had taken his side. A year later she was still angry, and she still didn’t know why they had cut her job.

Her cousin listened to the whole story and asked, “Did you ever tell him how you felt?” Lorraine looked at her. “How could I say that to my boss?”

Discussion
  1. Was it wrong for Lorraine to stay quiet? What did her silence protect? What did it cost?
  2. Was there a respectful way she could have told her manager how she felt?
  3. Where is Lorraine’s anger going now?

Section 2 · Teaching: four ways to handle conflict 30 min

  • Passive (stuffing it): you say nothing, give in, or walk away and stop talking. The message: your feelings matter, mine don’t. The anger doesn’t go away. It builds.
  • Aggressive (exploding): yelling, threats, put-downs, hitting. The message: my feelings matter, yours don’t.
  • Passive-aggressive (getting even sideways): the silent treatment, doing a job badly on purpose, gossip, sarcasm.
  • Assertive (speaking up with respect): saying what you feel and need, calmly and honestly, while respecting the other person. The message: your feelings matter, and so do mine.

Many Native cultures value quiet, patience and not interfering in other people’s business. Those are strengths. Being assertive doesn’t mean becoming loud or pushy. It means being able to say, when it really matters, “That hurt me,” before the hurt turns into a grudge or an explosion.

Five steps for working out a conflict (from Joe’s draft):

  1. Name the problem: what exactly happened?
  2. Name the feeling: hurt, frustrated, embarrassed?
  3. Name the impact: what did it cause?
  4. Decide: is this worth bringing up, or can I truly let it go?
  5. Talk it through: pick a calm time and say, “When ___ happened, I felt ___, because ___. What I’d like is ___.” Then listen.

Lesson 7

My toolbox

What will I do when anger rises?

Section 1 · Story: “Five Minutes at the Door” 25 min

Five Minutes at the Door

Mike worked twelve-hour shifts at the mine, two weeks on and two weeks off. On his first night home he walked in tired and sore. The dishes were piled up, the TV was loud, and the kids ran at him all talking at once.

Mike exploded. He yelled about the mess, the noise, everything. The kids scattered. His wife went quiet. In a strange way, Mike got what he needed: the house was silent. But nobody felt good, including him.

Later that night his wife said, “You need something when you walk in. What is it?” Mike thought about it. “Fifteen minutes. A shower. Something to eat. Then I can be a dad.”

The next time he came home, he hugged everyone and said, “Give me fifteen minutes, then I’m all yours.” He showered, ate a sandwich, and came out and played cards with the kids. Same need. Different way of meeting it.

Discussion
  1. What was Mike really needing?
  2. He got his quiet both times. What was different?
  3. What do you need when you walk in the door?

The timeout. Agree ahead of time, with your family or partner, that anyone can call a timeout. The person who calls it leaves for a set time, at least 20 minutes, to calm down, not to stew. Then they come back to finish the talk or set a time to finish it. Coming back is the important part.

Lesson 8

Making peace and walking on

How do I repair harm and keep going?

By the end: each person has a written anger plan, understands the difference between saying sorry and making things right, knows what to do after a slip, and leaves with a blessing and a certificate.

Section 1 · Story: “Leonard’s Letter” 25 min

Leonard’s Letter

Leonard was fifty-eight. For most of his son Danny’s childhood, Leonard had been angry, loud and sometimes rough. He had said “sorry” a hundred times. Then it would happen again. Danny was grown now, and they hardly spoke.

After finishing the group, Leonard wrote Danny a letter. He didn’t make excuses. He wrote down the things he had done that he remembered. He wrote that he knew it had made Danny afraid in his own home. He wrote that he was working on it and had a plan. He didn’t ask Danny for anything, not even forgiveness.

Danny didn’t answer. Weeks went by, then months. Leonard kept working his plan anyway.

In June, a text came: “Going to fish camp next weekend. You can come if you want.”

Discussion
  1. What made this letter different from Leonard’s hundred sorries?
  2. Why do you think he didn’t ask for anything?
  3. What if Danny had never answered? Was the letter still worth writing?

Section 2 · Teaching: repair, forgiveness and slips 30 min

Sorry isn’t the same as making it right. Being sorry is a feeling. Making things right means naming what you did, owning how it hurt, changing the behavior over time, and accepting that the other person may need a long time to trust you, or may never fully trust you again.

Forgiveness means letting go of the right to get even and handing the hurt to God. It doesn’t mean pretending it didn’t happen, and it doesn’t mean you must trust someone who is still unsafe. Holding onto bitterness keeps anger alive. Letting go frees you. (HWT Lesson 6 covers forgiveness in depth.)

Slips happen. Changing a lifelong habit takes time. If you blow up again, it doesn’t mean you failed. Do what you learned: take a timeout, figure out what set you off, make it right, and get back to your plan. If it keeps happening, reach out for more help.

Joe’s draft excludes people with active substance use problems. Also consider: current violence or a protective order, active suicidal thinking, and severe untreated mental illness. Each needs a referral path, not just a “no.”

5 · Native review of the stories

All eight stories were written for this draft. Robert and two or three Native facilitators (one Alaska Native, one from the Lower 48) should read them for anything that rings false or stereotyped. Regional versions may be worth it, since Robert noted that Lower 48 and Alaska Native experiences differ.

6 · Name

Keep Turning Away From Wrath (from Psalm 37:8)? It’s strong for church settings. For court and community settings, a plainer subtitle may help, such as “A path through anger.”

7 · Bible translation

ESV, to match the /anger-scripture resource. Joe’s draft mixes NIV wording. If you want NIV or NASB instead, it’s a quick swap.

8 · Facilitators

Same facilitators as Healing Groups, plus a one-day anger-specific training? Joe’s background makes him the natural trainer.

9 · Source check before publishing

Joe’s secular teaching closely follows SAMHSA’s Anger Management for Substance Use Disorder and Mental Health Clients (myths, group rules, timeouts, the four cue types, the conflict resolution model). That’s a strength: it is a tested 12-week model, listed as public domain (AHRQ listing; SAMHSA). It should be credited. The trigger checklist (“My spouse doesn’t load the dishwasher right…”) reads like it came from another book. If so, find the source before printing.

Part 5Cultural tailoring and research

On 12 Nov Joe asked whether there are real, studied differences in how Native and non-Native people express anger. Here is a first look. The short answer: there is very little research on anger specifically, but there are useful threads.

What we found

  • Very little has been studied directly. Most evidence-based treatments were not built with or tested on American Indian and Alaska Native people, and researchers keep calling for culturally adapted versions. We found no published outcome study of an anger management program adapted for Native participants. LIM would be breaking new ground. (Gray & Rose, J. Multicultural Counseling & Development)
  • Restraint and silence are often values, not deficits. Writers on Native counseling describe silence as valued in many communities, and people who are expressive in safe company but reserved when there is distrust. This supports Joe’s observation and Robert’s story, and it is why Lesson 6 treats quiet as a strength that sometimes needs a voice, not a problem to fix.
  • Holding feelings in has a health cost. A study in an American Indian community linked suppressing emotions with higher cardiovascular risk. That gives the “stuffing it” pattern a concrete cost participants can weigh. (PMC9764238)
  • Federal guidance on adapting programs. SAMHSA’s TIP 61, Behavioral Health Services for American Indians and Alaska Natives, was written with Native professionals. It also notes that not every client wants traditional practices in treatment, which is one more reason to ask rather than assume. (TIP 61)
  • A model that includes anger skills: the BRAVE program for Native teens and young adults teaches non-violent anger management alongside cultural values, and was designed with Native youth input. (Healthy Native Youth)
  • Historical trauma (Maria Yellow Horse Brave Heart’s work) and traditional ethics of non-interference and emotional restraint (Clare Brant, 1990, Canadian Journal of Psychiatry) are the two classic frames behind Lessons 2 and 6. Cited from memory; confirm before quoting.

Principles this draft follows

  1. Ask, don’t tell. Every lesson asks what participants’ own culture teaches. No lesson defines “Native culture.” This is Robert’s caution and HWT’s method.
  2. Native facilitators lead. At least one per group, following local tribal protocol as Healing Groups do.
  3. Name history without making it an excuse. Boarding schools and historical trauma explain patterns. They don’t excuse harm, and they don’t mean anyone is stuck.
  4. Hold faith gently. Some participants carry harm done in the name of the church. Scripture is offered as invitation and comfort, never as a weapon.
  5. Relevant for everyone. Robert wants it to work for all participants. The stories are set in Native communities, but the skills are universal.

Still to research

  • Talk with three or four Native facilitators and Elders, Alaska and Lower 48, using the culture questions in this draft, and note common themes (Robert’s suggestion).
  • Ask Alaska courts, tribal courts and probation in Fairbanks and Anchorage what they would require to accept the program.
  • Look at the full BRAVE evaluation and the Native Path to Courage trial for activities we could adapt.
  • Find out whether tribal health organizations (such as Southcentral Foundation, where Joe worked) have any internal anger or family-wellness curricula that could be shared.
