A History of Lutheran Indian Ministries

October 2026

Lutheran Indian Ministries began more than fifty years ago as a handful of pilots and pastors flying the Gospel into northern communities that no road reached. It is now a Native-led ministry that equips Native people to carry that Gospel to their own communities. This history traces how that change came about, what it cost, and where it has led.

Wings Over the North

1970–1984

On June 16, 1970, the Lutheran Association of Missionaries and Pilots (LAMP) was incorporated in Canada. Its founder, Rev. Les Stahlke, was a Lutheran pastor who combined his flying skills with Christian outreach to people in remote, sparsely settled areas of northern Alberta who had no pastoral care. Over the years LAMP added more pilot-pastors and extended its reach into the Northwest Territories, British Columbia, Alberta, Manitoba, Saskatchewan and Ontario.

LAMP built working relationships with the major denominations that had historically held spiritual responsibility for many northern communities. From those relationships grew a full-time volunteer corps, Christians-in-Service (CIS), whose members lived and worked in northern communities as a Christian presence, helping local service providers and assisting local churches.

In 1981 LAMP added the Volunteers-in-Ministry (VIM) program. Like CIS volunteers, VIM volunteers provided their own financial support, and over the years they came from all over North America. LAMP also sent Vacation Bible School teams, typically three to five teachers who spent one to two weeks in a northern Native community.

An American Partner

1985–1993

On January 3, 1985, Lutheran Association of Missionaries and Pilots U.S., Inc. was incorporated in the United States, with an office in Milwaukee, Wisconsin. Its purpose was to help Christian churches carry out ministry in the sparsely settled and physically isolated areas of Canada. In March 1989 the work expanded to Alaska.

LAMP now operated as two national organizations, one Canadian and one American, sharing a name, a logo and a mission field: a bush-aviation ministry carrying pastors into remote Native communities across northern Canada and Alaska with limited or no road access.

A Change of Conviction

1993–1999

Rev. Don Johnson, a Native American LCMS pastor, joined the staff in September 1993 and was appointed interim Executive Director in January 1994, the first Native American to lead the organization.

Under his leadership the emphasis shifted toward equipping Native American, First Nations and Alaska Native people to become spiritual leaders in their own communities. Beginning in 1995, and approved by both boards in 1996, a long-range plan reoriented the work from presence toward evangelism, discipleship, and the recruitment and training of Native Christians to serve as pastors to their own people. The vision was to disciple Native people for ministry, and Native Christians joined the staff.

That reorientation was not universally welcomed, and the two national organizations came to hold genuinely different convictions about how the work should be done.

The Separation

2000–2005

By 2000 the partnership had become unworkable. Joint board meetings no longer produced agreement, and staff of both LAMP U.S. and LAMP Canada were leaving. On April 25, 2000, the joint executive staff wrote to the U.S. board asking it to act. On May 5, 2000, the U.S. board voted to end the ministry partnership.

The U.S. organization's own work kept growing. As more Native leaders served their people through the ministry, it expanded in 2001 to the Makah Indian Reservation at Neah Bay, Washington, and later to the Lower Elwha and other tribes of the Olympic Peninsula.

Litigation followed over the shared name and marks. The federal court's orders were clear in their outcome: the U.S. organization could no longer use the acronym LAMP, though it kept its legal name, and its newsletter could no longer be called The LAMPLighter. A settlement order, still in force today, requires each organization to notify the other of any estate gift executed between January 1, 1985 and April 1, 2005. Fewer gifts fall inside that window with each passing year.

One Ministry, Many Nations

2006–2015

In 2006, Lutheran Association of Missionaries and Pilots U.S. merged with the Council for Lutheran American Indian Ministries (CLAIM), bringing Lutheran ministry among Native Americans under a single organization. Alongside the existing work in Alaska, Canada and the Olympic Peninsula, the merger added ministry in Kansas, through Haskell LIGHT, and in New Mexico, through Shepherd of the Valley Lutheran Church in Navajo.

In March 2010 the ministry adopted a new name that better reflected its mission and vision: Lutheran Indian Ministries (LIM). Its legal name remains Lutheran Association of Missionaries and Pilots U.S., Inc. In 2014 LIM extended its work to Hawaii, serving Native Hawaiian people.

Transition and Testing

2016–2021

Don Johnson retired in 2016 after more than two decades. Tim Young Eagle (Pawnee), a former board member and long-time advocate of Native American outreach, succeeded him as Executive Director and served from 2016 to 2020. In 2018 the ministry expanded to the many tribes living in and around Phoenix, Arizona.

Under Tim's leadership LIM moved into urban Native communities and began adding social and healing ministry to its work. It affirmed the diversity of God's creation and the unity all believers share in Christ, encouraging Native Christians to use every gift God has given them, including their languages and cultures, to glorify their Savior, Jesus Christ.

An effort to offer fee-for-service programs in Phoenix brought the hardest chapter in the organization's recent history. LIM contracted with outside billing agencies to handle Medicaid claims. Those agencies were committing fraud, without LIM's knowledge. The organization and several staff were charged. LIM and its Chief Financial Officer were dismissed from the charges, and a portion of the fee-for-service revenue was returned to Medicaid.

Robert Heffle was hired in December [[2021]], in the middle of that litigation, and brought all court proceedings with the State of Arizona to a settlement.

A New Footing

Recent years

When the requirements for LCMS Recognized Service Organizations (RSOs) changed to call for a board made up entirely of LCMS members, LIM was not renewed as an RSO. Without that status LIM could no longer call pastors, and the change led it to rethink its role in Native missions.

Its focus turned to supporting the LCMS congregations and pastors who serve Native communities. LIM began an annual Pastor Gathering to support pastors, solve problems together and hear their needs. Rather than calling a pastor to Neah Bay, LIM now supports the congregation there with a direct financial contribution, while the pastor is supervised by the Northwest District and the congregation's board.

Healing the Wounds of Trauma

2022 onward

In 2022 the LIM board approved a focus on the trauma that affects Native communities and families, with the aim of offering Christ-centered programs to meet that need. LIM adopted Healing the Wounds of Trauma: How the Church Can Help, from the American Bible Society's Trauma Healing Institute.

The first trainings were held in Fairbanks and Anchorage, Alaska. Those trained, including LIM staff, an LCMS pastor and an Elder, went on to lead in-person healing groups in cities, villages and reservations. When invited, LIM holds a healing group, trains local facilitators, and offers ongoing support virtually and in person. Healing groups are now also offered online, so that people can experience Christ-centered healing and learn to help others in their own communities who have experienced trauma.

Into the Word

2026

In 2026 LIM invited people to read through the Bible in a Year together, with a reading guide and a general schedule. Sixteen people signed up in the first week and 91 in all, and 8 to 15 take part in each bi-weekly online meeting. The group expects to finish reading the entire Bible in March 2027. Next, LIM plans to invite a group to read through the New Testament, with context for each chapter offered by an LCMS pastor.

Where the Ministry Stands

Today

LIM today is a Native-led ministry organized around three commitments: to proclaim the Gospel in Native communities, to disciple Native leaders, most visibly through Haskell LIGHT, and to heal the wounds of trauma in Native communities and families.

That third commitment has become some of the ministry's most consequential work. Its healing groups, built on the Trauma Healing Institute curriculum and led by trained Native facilitators, meet in person and online at no cost to those who attend.

LIM continues to proclaim the Gospel of Jesus Christ among Native American people, equipping Nations with the Word of God and with resources that help heal social suffering, while valuing Native cultures. Its message to Native people is that even the wounded can be used by God to reach others, and that generational change is possible.
